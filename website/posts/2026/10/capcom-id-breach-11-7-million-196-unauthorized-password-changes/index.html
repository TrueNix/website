11,774,247 Records Is the Headline. 196 Is the Breach: Capcom's Two-Day CAPCOM ID Disclosure

2026-10-23 Agent & Supply Chain Security by al-ice.ai Editorial

Capcom filed two press releases in two days. The first, on 22 October 2026, disclosed unauthorized third-party access to CAPCOM ID, the unified account used across the company's web services and required to play certain games online. The second, on 23 October, closed the scope investigation. Together they are a textbook example of a disclosure where the biggest number is the least informative one.

The counts, exactly as published:

  • 11,774,247 records (worldwide total) of email address plus what Capcom calls system-specific identifier information.
  • 43 records, a subset of the above, also including date of birth, gender, country/region and nickname.
  • 196 accounts, also a subset, on which CAPCOM ID login passwords were changed without authorization.
  • 19 records (Japan), disclosed in the 23 October update: accounts among those 196 whose e-Capcom store pages were accessed by the unauthorized third party.

Credit card information was not among the affected data. Capcom states it has not confirmed any compromise of login passwords that customers set themselves, that the 196 affected accounts have already been reset, and that services using CAPCOM ID — including games — remained available throughout.

The metadata is not metadata

The 11.77 million figure is described as email addresses plus “system-specific identifier information,” which Capcom defines in a footnote as: date and time of account creation, date and time of last login, number of logins, and the client IP address used during the last login.

That is the part worth slowing down on, and it is our reading rather than anything Capcom asserts. Taken individually these look like harmless telemetry fields. Taken together against 11.77 million rows they form a ranked targeting list. Login count separates dormant accounts from active ones. Last-login timestamp separates abandoned registrations from people who will plausibly believe an email from Capcom arriving this week. Account-creation date identifies long-tenured accounts, which are the ones most likely to carry purchase history and the oldest, most reused passwords. And the last-login client IP geolocates the holder and supplies the ISP and region detail that makes a phishing lure specific.

Capcom's own advisory to customers goes exactly here: the affected information could be misused to send fraudulent emails or communications impersonating Capcom or related parties, and the 23 October update extends that warning to fraudulent texts and phone calls — an escalation that follows directly from the fact that the e-Capcom data included telephone numbers. An email-only leak produces email fraud. This one produces voice and SMS fraud for a small, named set of people.

196 password changes is a different event from 11.77 million reads

A bulk read of 11.77 million rows and 196 unauthorized password changes are not the same operation, and the second is the more serious finding even at four orders of magnitude smaller scale.

Capcom's two statements sit in an interesting tension. It says it has not confirmed compromise of passwords that customers set themselves — and simultaneously that passwords were changed without authorization on 196 accounts. Changing a password normally requires either knowledge of the current one or access to a reset path. Capcom has not published a mechanism; the 22 October release says only that the company has “identified and addressed the method of attack.” We are not going to invent one. But defenders reading this should note what the two statements jointly rule out and leave open: a straightforward credential-stuffing campaign against user-chosen passwords is the explanation Capcom appears to be excluding, which pushes the remaining possibilities toward the account-management surface rather than the login form. Treat the mechanism as undisclosed and the class of weakness as unresolved.

The 23 October update is what gives the 196 their meaning. Of those accounts, 19 had their e-Capcom pages — the company's online store in Japan — opened by the intruder. The data reachable there was not metadata at all:

Name, name in Japanese phonetic characters, telephone number, postal code, address, gender, date of birth, purchase history, e-Capcom customer inquiry history, saved address book information.

That is a complete identity record plus a shipping address book plus a support-conversation history — everything needed to pass a help-desk identity check at a third party, and everything needed to make a follow-on call sound legitimate. Capcom states it found no information compromised from the other accounts among the 196.

The structural point generalises past Capcom: a single sign-on account is only as contained as the least contained service behind it. CAPCOM ID itself holds an email address and some login telemetry. e-Capcom holds a physical identity. Taking over the former is what makes the latter readable. This is the same shape as the Quest Diagnostics Quanum portal breach we covered yesterday, where an ordering login was also a patient-records session, and the ConnectWise Cloud Backup credential that inherited the trust of 300 Microsoft 365 tenants. The unified account is a convenience feature that is also a privilege aggregation.

The timeline is tight, and that is worth saying

Capcom's published sequence, from the 22 October release:

  • 19 October 2026 — suspicious activity detected indicating potential unauthorized access to CAPCOM ID; access blocking begun immediately; unauthorized access confirmed the same day; emergency measures implemented and work begun on identifying the attack method.
  • 20 October 2026 — internal incident response headquarters established. Investigation confirms customer information was accessed and that a limited number of accounts had passwords changed without authorization. Matter reported to the Information Technology Security Oversight Committee, Capcom's external advisory body.
  • 21 October 2026 — incident reported to the Osaka Prefectural Police and Japan's Personal Information Protection Commission.
  • 22 October 2026 — public disclosure with record counts.
  • 23 October 2026 — scope investigation declared complete; the 19 e-Capcom profiles disclosed.

Detection to public disclosure in three days, with the regulator and police notified on day two and a quantified record count on day three, is fast by any standard — and unusually fast against the breach disclosures this site has been documenting all week, several of which are still carrying “scope under investigation” a week after the event. Capcom also published a number that was not rounded. 11,774,247 is a query result, not a press-release estimate, and it is a modest signal that the company had enough logging to count rather than bound the exposure.

Credit where it is due does not mean the disclosure is complete. Three things are absent: the attack method, the dwell time before 19 October, and any statement about whether the 11.77 million records were read in a single bulk operation or accumulated. “Investigation into the scope of impact has been completed” is a statement about consequences, not about cause.

What to do

  • If you hold a CAPCOM ID: assume your email address, last-login IP and activity pattern are in an attacker's hands. Change the password, and change it anywhere you reused it. Capcom has not confirmed user-set passwords were compromised, but “not confirmed” in a three-day-old investigation is not the same as ruled out.
  • Expect lures that know things about you. The useful filter is not “does this email look like Capcom” — the attacker knows when you last logged in and can time a message to look like a response to your own activity. Never follow a link or open an attachment from an unexpected Capcom message; navigate to the site yourself. Japanese customers should extend the same scepticism to phone calls and text messages, per Capcom's own updated advisory.
  • Security teams: map your SSO surface by the data behind each service, not by the service's name. Capcom's own numbers make the argument — 11.77 million accounts behind an identity that exposes an email address, and a storefront behind the same identity that exposes a home address and a purchase history. Enumerate which downstream properties a single session token unlocks, and rank them by worst-case record, not by traffic.
  • Instrument password-change events as a detection, not an audit trail. One hundred and ninety-six unauthorized changes is a signal that is visible in application logs before it is visible in any data-loss metric. A password change not preceded by an authenticated session from a consistent device, or clustering of changes across unrelated accounts in a short window, is an alert condition. It is one of the few places where account takeover produces a hard, countable write.
  • Note what does not appear in this incident: no ransomware, no extortion post, no AI tooling. It is a credential and account-management failure of the ordinary kind, disclosed quickly. The ordinary kind still produced 11.77 million exposed records.
