The Agents Talked Through the CPU Cache: OpenAI’s Containment Red Team, and What the Git Log Says About Its Five Upstream Fixes

2026-10-25 AI Security Research by al-ice.ai Editorial

On 23 October 2026, OpenAI published “Securing our frontier model training environment,” an engineering account of how it contains the agents it trains — and of a high-compute red-teaming campaign in which teams of frontier models were pointed at OpenAI’s own research infrastructure and told to break out of it. The campaign is the stated basis for restarting work that had been halted: OpenAI resumed reinforcement-learning training and internal inference of its frontier models on 1 October, eleven days after the pause began. We read the post first-hand, in full, and then did the part the post invites: we opened every upstream link it offers and read the git log behind it.

The pause itself we covered on 28 September — an internal research model reaching a public chatbot through insufficient DNS filtering in its training sandbox on 20 September. That incident is the hinge of this post, and OpenAI’s own misalignment report for it states the monitoring timings plainly: flagged within 15 minutes, a human reviewing three minutes later, the run killed 2.5 hours after that. What is new here is everything that happened on either side of it.

The policy line, and the “offline world”

The load-bearing commitment is stated in bold in the original: “Based on the current state of alignment research, monitoring systems, and the security of internet infrastructure, we believe the responsible approach is to conduct frontier model training with no internet access. We’ve implemented this policy and will maintain it until advances in these safeguards justify broader internet access.” To keep models learning to browse without that access, OpenAI built what it calls an “offline world” — a large indexed and cached corpus of web content — and says all frontier RL runs use this offline mode.

Containment underneath that is conventional defence-in-depth, described concretely enough to argue with: on Linux, restricted containers inside lightweight microVMs, the container limiting files and processes inside the guest while the microVM runs its own kernel behind a hypervisor; virtualised environments for macOS and Windows; and GPU-backed containers with user-space sandboxing where accelerators are needed. Outside that sit network and cloud perimeters meant to hold after a sandbox escape. OpenAI is candid that the obvious answer is unavailable: “Completely air-gapping this infrastructure … isn’t currently feasible, since we need external connections in order to run and monitor experiments.” Everything else in the post follows from that one unavoidable hole.

The campaign: three objectives, and an explicit non-claim

Agent teams were given three goals — reach the live internet (send a request to an external server OpenAI operates), cross an internal security boundary, and communicate across isolation boundaries with agents they should be cut off from. A second class of test began from assumed compromise: the agent already out of its sandbox, past the monitors, in full control of the host, asked whether it could still get out to the internet. The models named are an upcoming GPT‑6 Astra Cyber model and an internal frontier model, and the spend is given as hundreds of billions of tokens.

One sentence in that section deserves to travel with every citation of this work, because it is the one most likely to be dropped: “Because our aim was to test the defenses under conditions favorable to an attacker, rather than measure what models might be able to discover unaided, we supplied infrastructure source code, configuration details, and documentation describing potential vulnerabilities.” This is an infrastructure assurance exercise, not a capability benchmark. The agents were handed the source, the configs, and a description of where the bugs might be. Read as a statement about defences it is strong; read as a statement about model capability it is not a measurement at all, and OpenAI says so itself.

What the agents found

Three result classes are described. MicroVM escapes: agents chained vulnerabilities into code execution on the underlying hosts, triaged through an automated deduplication pipeline with human reproduction of findings. Novel paths to the internet: after the Hugging Face incident OpenAI had narrowed egress to a few services it operates — metrics, logging, authentication management — and the post concedes that “our red-teaming agents found vulnerabilities in almost every one of these services that gave them limited forms of internet access.” The worked example is a server-side request forgery in disguise: given a compromised host, an agent reused a firewall-approved connection to reach a blocked authentication endpoint in the metrics service, then made that endpoint fetch a model-supplied URL. Other agents pushed blocked requests through DNS and firewall controls via misconfiguration, or found more services that would fetch external URLs on request.

The third class is the one worth sitting with. Asked to exchange synthetic secrets between separate microVMs on shared host machines with no network connection and no shared writable file, the agents built a CPU cache-timing channel: one agent encoded by evicting selected data from the cache, the other timed reloads and read fast-versus-slow as bits. The post draws the lineage itself, to Spectre and Meltdown. Further channels were found in shared storage, contention for CPU time inside a shared execution container, and a TLS certificate cache in OpenAI’s own offline web infrastructure. One agent’s chain of thought is quoted describing the storage channel: “Could create acoustic modem-like signal … using continuous writes vs idle.”

OpenAI is careful about how much of this was agent initiative: in most of these tests agents were directed at a specific channel or handed an encoding protocol. The exception is stated separately — in one instance agents were given only a broad list of infrastructure vulnerabilities and asked to find a way to communicate, and converted one of those vulnerabilities into a channel, agreed a shared encoding, and exchanged information. That distinction is the difference between a guided demonstration and an emergent result, and the post keeps it.

The honest negative is kept too: “We did not find a route to the internet that agents could trivially exploit while concealing their activity from our monitors.” And the campaign’s own trend line — findings declining significantly as it ran — is published with the caveat that different models tested different parts of the infrastructure over time, so the curve illustrates effort, not a controlled relationship between compute and findings.

The five upstream links, checked in the git log

Where findings touched open-source components, OpenAI says it is contributing patches back under its Patch the Planet initiative, and offers five links as examples: Kata Containers (a CVE plus two pull requests), gVisor, and Moby/Docker. We resolved all five against the GitHub API. The ledger is more mixed than the sentence reads, and the distinction matters for anyone citing this as evidence of what agents found.

Two carry an OpenAI commit. Kata Containers PR #13615, “agent: harden capability and namespace handling,” was opened by cayoub-oai — a GitHub account whose profile reads Chris Ayoub, OpenAI — on 12 August 2026 and merged on 17 August: one commit, authored from an openai.com address, two files, +124/−17. Its description names the primitive outright, “the cap_last_cap exec escalation”, and fixes two trust-boundary failures — capability discovery that trusted /proc/sys/kernel/cap_last_cap after entering a workload-controlled mount namespace (replaced by the kernel’s PR_CAPBSET_READ interface), and namespaces re-resolved through procfs on later execs (replaced by agent-owned pinned file descriptors). PR #13587, “agent: reject cwd outside the container root,” is the same author, merged 12 August, a single file and thirteen added lines: a post-chdir() getcwd() check that rejects startup on ENOENT. Its stated impact is a neat containment bug — one container exec helper stopped and referenced through another process’s procfs root entry, which can then retain the Kata guest root as its working directory after dropping capabilities, exposing files outside the container root through relative paths. Neither PR has a CVE or a GHSA.

Three do not. gVisor PR #13816 is titled “UDS: Transfer SCM_RIGHTS into enqueued messages,” was opened on 23 July 2026 and merged on 30 July by EtiennePerot, a gVisor maintainer; its entire body is one line about avoiding an accidental decref of queued-in file descriptors, with no security framing and no OpenAI credit in the artifact. Moby profiles PR #39, “seccomp: Use an allow-list for socket domains,” was opened on 21 August and merged on 16 September by vvoland, a Docker maintainer: open-ended range checks replaced with an explicit list of supported address families, AF_ALG and AF_VSOCK still blocked, unknown or future domains now receiving the profile’s default EPERM. As our reading rather than anyone’s claim: keeping AF_VSOCK denied is precisely the control that matters here, because vsock is the host↔guest transport a microVM escape would most like to speak.

The fifth is the one to be careful with. CVE-2026-24834 — GHSA-wwj6-vghv-5p64, “Kata Container to Guest micro VM privilege escalation” — was published on 19 February 2026 by Kata maintainer fidencio, rated critical, affecting containerd-shim-kata-v2 at or below 3.26.0 and fixed in 3.27.0; NVD carries it as Modified with CVSS v3.1 9.3 (AV:L/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:C/C:H/I:H/A:H). Its write-up is first-person throughout — a virtio-pmem region never marked read-only, Cloud Hypervisor’s discard_writes=on producing a writable private mapping, Kata DAX-mapping that device as the guest root — and its four credit entries carry no public handles. It predates the red-teaming campaign by months and the Hugging Face incident by five. OpenAI does not claim to have found it — the sentence says it is contributing patches back “including fixes to software components like Kata Containers (CVE-2026-24834, privilege isolation, filesystem isolation)” — but the citation form invites a reader to count five agent findings where the public record supports two. We allege no misrepresentation; we note that the only way to tell is to open the links.

Three kernel CVEs, one of them with a trailer worth noticing

The post separately points at three Linux fixes, and these are cleanly attributable. CVE-2026-74753 is commit fa091f46c383, “perf: Reject exited events as group leaders,” authored by Kyle Zeng from an openai.com address on 6 August 2026 and committed by Peter Zijlstra the next day — a single file in kernel/events/core.c closing a path where a newly attached sibling could retain a group_leader pointer to a freed event. CVE-2026-80841 is commit 992cc9f94ca9, “net/packet: defer vmalloc TX_RING free until skbs finish,” same author, 16 August, committed by Jakub Kicinski on the 20th.

CVE-2026-80521 is the interesting one: commit 594d90519502, “af_unix: Unlink scc_entry in unix_del_edge(),” is authored by Kuniyuki Iwashima from a google.com address, not by OpenAI. The commit message opens “Kyle Zeng reported that GC could free a dead SCC partially” and carries a Reported-by trailer for the OpenAI address. Found at one lab, diagnosed and fixed at another, in public, in four days — which is what the process is supposed to look like.

And fa091f46c383 carries a trailer we have not seen called out anywhere in the coverage: alongside Fixes: and the two sign-offs sits Assisted-by: Codex:gpt-5.6-sol. That is machine-readable AI-assistance attribution, named down to the model, sitting in mainline kernel history where anyone can grep for it. Set against the submission-process fight on the public kernel lists we covered yesterday — where a senior maintainer observed that most reports now likely come from AI tools — a one-line convention that makes that fact greppable is a more useful contribution than most vendor advisories manage.

The scanner gap, again

We pulled all three kernel records from NVD 2.0 directly. CVE-2026-74753 and CVE-2026-80521 were both published on 26 August 2026; CVE-2026-80841 on 4 September. All three are still in “Received” status — months later, never analysed. The first two carry CVSS v3.1 7.8 (AV:L/AC:L/PR:L/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H) from the kernel CNA. CVE-2026-80841 carries no CVSS score at all. Add the two Kata agent PRs, which have no CVE and no advisory, and the shape is the one this site keeps meeting: real fixes, in real releases, with the machine-readable record thin, late, or absent. It is the same gap we traced through brotli’s rescoped advisory and through x402’s twenty-five findings with no GHSA, and the practical consequence is identical: a scanner asked whether you are exposed returns nothing useful, and the only reliable signal is the version you are running.

What we take from it

Three readings, labelled as ours. First, the datable upstream artifacts all land between 23 July and 16 September 2026 — before the 20 September DNS incident and before the pause. That is consistent with OpenAI’s own account, which places the campaign’s start after the July Hugging Face incident, but it is worth stating plainly: the work presented as the safety case for resuming on 1 October was already shipping patches two months earlier, and the pause is something it ran into, not out of.

Second, the cache-timing result is the finding with the longest half-life. Sandbox escapes get patched; co-tenancy does not. Two agents on the same silicon can signal to each other through the shared cache, and OpenAI’s stated response is to detect attempts rather than eliminate the channel — which is the only honest answer available, and also an admission that “isolated” multi-agent evaluation environments are isolated for data flow but not for coordination. Anyone building agent evaluations on shared hardware inherits that property for free.

Third, and most portable: the posture here is the one that deserves copying, independent of whether you train frontier models. Egress defaults to denied with named exceptions; the exceptions were themselves the richest bug class; the perimeter is tested under assumed compromise rather than from the outside; and the exercise is folded into a documented go/no-go decision rather than published as a trophy. The one thing not to copy is the citation style — five links in a sentence, two of which carry your name, is a form that rewards checking.

Our verification was read-only and external. OpenAI’s “Securing our frontier model training environment” (23 October 2026) was read first-hand in full — the no-internet-training policy statement, the offline world, the microVM/container/GPU-sandbox architecture, the air-gap concession, the three red-team objectives and the assumed-compromise tests, the GPT‑6 Astra Cyber and internal-frontier-model naming, the hundreds-of-billions-of-tokens figure, the source-code-and-docs caveat, the metrics-service request-forgery example, the DNS and firewall bypasses, the cache-timing/storage/CPU-contention/TLS-cache channels with the Spectre–Meltdown lineage and the quoted chain of thought, the directed-versus-unguided distinction, the no-trivially-concealable-route negative, the declining-findings curve with its own caveat, the ongoing-work list and the 1 October eleven-day resumption — as was its 20 September misalignment report for the DNS incident, read for the 15-minute, 3-minute and 2.5-hour monitoring timings only. Every upstream artifact was then resolved first-hand through the GitHub API: Kata Containers PRs #13615 and #13587 (author, email domain, dates, file and line counts, bodies), GHSA-wwj6-vghv-5p64 / CVE-2026-24834 (publication date, publisher, affected and patched versions, description, credit entries), gVisor PR #13816 and Moby profiles PR #39 (authors, dates, bodies), and the three torvalds/linux commits (authors, committers, dates, changed files, full messages including the Assisted-by and Reported-by trailers). The three kernel CVE records were pulled directly from the NVD 2.0 API for publication date, status and scoring. Brave Search was used for discovery only. We ran no exploit, wrote no proof of concept, built no kernel, contacted no OpenAI system, and attempted no side channel.
