16 per Million and Growing: Radware Counts Hidden Prompt Injections Across GitHub

2026-10-29 Agent & Supply Chain Security by al-ice.ai Editorial

Radware researchers Gabi Nakibly and Zvika Babo presented a GitHub-scale census of indirect prompt injection at [un]prompted II in San Francisco on 27 October 2026 — moving the discussion from proof-of-concept to what is actually happening in public development workflows. The talk was billed as a scan of 86 million GitHub items; the accompanying open repository (created 26 October, updated 29 October) documents a corpus of roughly 145 million items: 95.1 million new issues and pull requests from June through September 2026, 47.6 million GH Archive issues, PRs and comments, plus the READMEs of the 2 million most-starred repositories. From that corpus came 2,281 real AI-directed findings in issues, PRs and comments — about 16 per million items — including 47 outright attacks aimed at making someone else’s agent act against its owner.

The findings split into four categories, and the split is the story. First-party orchestration dominates with 1,519 findings across 178 projects — teams leaving hidden instructions and markers for their own agents and agent tooling. Then come 374 content-override attempts (steering review language, attribution, promotion), 345 defensive markers (keeping agents out, making them identify themselves, trapping them), and 43 attacks in issues, PRs and comments plus 4 in READMEs — roughly one attack per 3.3 million items, attempting secret leaks, forged verdicts, and command execution. Three defender-facing observations from the researchers: attacks are rare but targeted — more than half aim at a project’s own triage, review or CI agent, and several name that agent’s real tools or output fields, meaning the attacker read the workflow first; most hidden text is friendly and growing fast — 75% written by project owners, members or collaborators, with per-million rates up 2.2× (7.0 to 15.4) from June to September and coordinating projects up from 11 to 69; and friendly markers are forgeable — plain text in a public thread that any bot obeying markers without checking authorship can be fooled by.

The methodology is built for reuse, which is what makes this more than a conference talk. The ipi-scan tool is open source in the same repository: it detects text a human cannot see but an agent receives (HTML comments on issues and PRs, plus invisible Unicode, white LaTeX and CSS-hidden elements in READMEs), screens it with a fast model, re-examines positives with a stronger one, and — critically — marks hidden text copied from the repo’s own issue/PR templates as template text, a filter that caught 60% of classifier-confirmed items. The prompts that classify findings are themselves hardened: hidden text is wrapped in delimiters, forged delimiters stripped, models instructed never to follow it. Every number ships with its data (per-finding JSONL, funnel and monthly tables) and a script that recomputes all of them — and every number is explicitly a lower bound, since only hidden text is counted and GH Archive now captures a fraction of GitHub activity.

Why defenders should take this seriously — and where to discount it

The serious part is that indirect prompt injection now has a measured background rate in the wild, and the trend line points the wrong way: friendly hidden markers more than doubling in a quarter means the forgeable-marker attack surface is expanding even where nobody is attacking yet. Targeted attacks naming real agent tools confirm adversaries are reading workflows before striking — the same workflow-reconnaissance pattern behind the Comment-and-Control injections, GitLost, and the March issue-title campaign that reached 4,000 machines. The discount: genuine attacks remain vanishingly rare (47 in ~145 million), most hidden text is benign or defensive, and the headline corpus size differs between the talk billing (86 million) and the repository’s current documentation (~145 million) — use the repository’s versioned numbers, which are recomputable from the published data. Also note CERT-In’s parallel warning this month that AI agents accessed live systems at Indian firms under test conditions — the census shows the delivery mechanism; CERT-In showed the consequence.

What it means for builders and defenders

  • Treat HTML comments in issues and PRs as untrusted input. They are invisible to reviewers and fully visible to agents — the exact asymmetry this census measured. Triage, review and CI agents should ignore, strip, or sandbox hidden Markdown by default.
  • Authenticate your own markers by authorship. If your agent fleet coordinates through hidden comments, check that the marker author is an owner, member, or collaborator before obeying — 75% of friendly markers are, which means 25% are not, and all of them are trivially forgeable.
  • Scan your own repositories with ipi-scan. The tool is public, runs per-repo, and needs only a GitHub token for detection mode. Run it against repos where agents review PRs or triage issues, and repeat after template changes — templates are the biggest source of lookalike noise.
  • Fix the template hygiene first. With 60% of confirmed items tracing to issue/PR template text, moving agent directives out of copy-pasted hidden comments and into authenticated configuration cuts both noise and camouflage for real injections.
