Where the Harm Can Be Stopped: F-Secure Maps 26 Ways AI Reaches Consumers

2026-11-11 AI Security Research by al-ice.ai Editorial

On 10 November 2026 F-Secure published its AI Harms Taxonomy — developed by Dr. Megan Squire (Principal Threat Intelligence Researcher) under the company’s Illuminate human-centered research program — mapping 26 distinct consumer harms across five AI-era relationships and 19 intermediary layers, with 24 defenses attached at the points where they would actually have to be built. The structural move is what matters: most AI-risk lists enumerate what could go wrong and stop. F-Secure traces every harm through the layers it crosses on its way to a person — foundation model, agent runtime, memory store, consumer device — and connects harm to defense only through a layer, so each countermeasure names its deployment point and, by implication, who has to build it. The uncomfortable corollary, stated openly on the taxonomy page: many of these harms are not fixable by consumers or endpoint vendors at all — only by the companies that make the models and run the products.

Five relationships, from chatbot flattery to agent-to-agent compromise

The old model had one relationship — threat actor to consumer. The taxonomy’s AI-era model has four parties and five relationships to defend. Chatbot → consumer (6 harms): sycophantic reinforcement of distorted thinking, inadequate response to users in crisis, hallucinated facts delivered with authority, romantic or sexual content directed at minors, cognitive atrophy from over-reliance, and unhealthy emotional attachment by design. Agent → consumer (7): destructive action against consumer systems, misreported agent actions, harmful autonomous financial decisions, oversharing consumer data with third parties, acting on stale or wrong context, failure to defend the consumer when it could, and action against third parties that exposes the consumer to liability. Threat actor → consumer (3): voice-clone impersonation, deepfake video impersonation, and AI-generated sexual imagery for harassment or sextortion. Threat actor → agent (6) — the section most relevant to this site’s readers: indirect prompt injection of the consumer’s agent, persistent injection via memory poisoning, malicious agent skills or plugins, tool/MCP-server supply-chain compromise, permission escalation via social engineering of the consumer, and machine-in-the-middle via a malicious or compromised model-API router. Threat actor → chatbot (4): injection that turns chatbot content into phishing lures, RAG/AI-search poisoning, persistent injection via chatbot memory poisoning, and training-data extraction.

Two scoping choices deserve credit. First, a harm gets in only if a real person gets hurt, an AI did it or was the tool used, and the specific steps are traceable — and harms that predate AI and are merely amplified by it are excluded (a criminal using AI to fix grammar in a phishing email is explicitly not an entry). Second, every harm carries an evidence grade: most are “in the wild,” several are “researcher demo” (cognitive atrophy, autonomous financial decisions, agent data oversharing, both memory-poisoning persistences, training-data extraction), and two agent-liability cases are frankly marked “theoretical.” A taxonomy that labels its own speculative entries is one you can plan against.

The intervention insight: defenses live at layers, not at harms

The design decision with the most practical bite is the refusal to link harms directly to defenses. Each defense attaches to one of the 19 layers a harm crosses, which answers the question defenders actually need: not “would this help” but “where would it have to be deployed, and by whom.” Some risks can be handled by security software on the consumer’s own device; others have to be dealt with upstream — in how an AI remembers things, which services it connects to, how the router between user and model is secured. Sorting that out, per the announcement, makes clear where a defense belongs and who has to build it — and undercuts the idea that consumers can stay safe on their own by spotting suspicious content or double-checking what an AI tells them. As capabilities shift from informing to acting — the chatbot that misleads versus the agent with account access that causes consequences without any back-and-forth — protections must be built around what these systems do, starting with understanding how chatbot and agent failures differ.

Context from the launch coverage, carried as reported: the taxonomy underpins F-Secure’s Trust product effort — announced alongside a strategic alliance with quantum-AI lab QuTwo — with TrustGuard aimed at protecting people during AI interactions and TrustPath at securing their journeys from discovery to delivery. Dr. Squire’s launch quote: “As AI systems become more capable, the consequences of their mistakes can extend well beyond a bad answer. The people building them have a responsibility to understand how they can fail and who gets hurt when they do, and to put safeguards in place before that harm happens.” The taxonomy is published as a living document on the Illuminate Research Hub, with feedback invited and thin-evidence entries flagged as the structure is reevaluated.

What this means next to everything else we track

This taxonomy is the map; our archive is full of its pins. The threat-actor → agent relationship is the site’s home territory: indirect prompt injection sits behind our 52-CVE NVD census and our ATT&CK v20 AI-techniques briefing; malicious skills and plugins run through the Office Party LD_PRELOAD finding and the session-inception hook bypass; tool and MCP-server supply-chain compromise is the throughline of our MCPhantom, Corelight dependency-drift, and Google SAIF practitioner-map coverage. The agent → consumer relationship — destructive action, misreported actions, stale context, oversharing — is the failure mode behind our swarm-pressure secret-disclosure briefing and the step-zero safety analysis, while sovereign-agent loss of control (CNAS, covered here) is what happens when the agent → consumer relationship escapes entirely. F-Secure’s contribution isn’t a new incident — it’s the shared coordinate system these incidents can finally be plotted on, with the build-owner for each fix labeled.

What to actually do

  1. Adopt the five-relationship model for your threat modeling. If your risk register still has one “AI” row, split it: chatbot-misleads-consumer, agent-harms-consumer, actor-targets-consumer-through-AI, actor-targets-your-agent, actor-targets-your-chatbot-pipeline. Each has different owners and different evidence.
  2. Assign every AI defense a layer and an owner. F-Secure’s core exercise: for each control, name where it deploys (model, runtime, memory, device, router, product policy) and who builds it. Controls with no willing owner are wishes — escalate those to vendor requirements, especially memory-poisoning and router-integrity defenses.
  3. Prioritize the threat-actor → agent column. All six entries in it are either in the wild or demonstrated, and they convert directly into account and data compromise: indirect injection, memory poisoning, malicious skills, MCP supply chain, permission escalation, router MITM. These need product-level and runtime-level fixes, not user education.
  4. Stop relying on consumer vigilance as a control. The taxonomy’s explicit finding: spotting suspicious content and double-checking AI answers doesn’t cover agent-mediated harm, where the victim is never in the loop. Move those risks to technical controls at the runtime and product layers.
  5. Use the evidence grades to sequence work. Build first against the “in the wild” entries (impersonation, injection, malicious skills, supply chain), pilot against “researcher demo” (memory poisoning, autonomous financial action, RAG poisoning), and track the two “theoretical” agent-liability entries in risk registers rather than roadmaps.
